## Post completo

One private credit fund just agreed to sell itself at 90% of NAV. The buyer pays with shares valued at 100%.

The facts: Investcorp Credit Management BDC will merge into BCP Investment Corporation, managed by BC Partners. Shareholders get 90% of adjusted NAV, in stock and cash. Closing is expected in Q1 2027. The same day, the buyer reported its own NAV down to $13.13 per share from $14.49 in June, after markdowns. (Source: company release; Bloomberg)

Put this next to the other model in the market.

Listed BDCs: priced every day, often at a discount to NAV. When they consolidate, the discount becomes the deal price.

Non-traded and semi-liquid funds: subscriptions and redemptions at NAV, with quarterly caps. This year several of them have faced record redemption requests.

Same asset class, two prices for similar loans. A merger at 90% of NAV is not a market verdict on every portfolio. But it is a data point that LPs, auditors and boards of semi-liquid funds will find hard to ignore.

My read: consolidation of small listed BDCs will continue, and each deal will make the gap with semi-liquid NAVs more visible.

If two private credit funds merge at 90% of NAV, what should a semi-liquid fund holding similar loans report as its NAV?
